So where is all this, actually? Because "Temple's getting data centers" makes it sound like they're building one downtown. They're not. Meta sits on the industrial east side off Industrial Boulevard. The Rowan projects run along Bob White Road on the south side and out toward FM 3117. `[FILM-DAY: confirm each site's roadway description on the current map]` These are edge-of-town industrial corridors — which is exactly why the fight is really about the handful of streets that used to be the edge of town and aren't anymore.

### CH 3 — THE RECEIPTS: WHAT PRICES ACTUALLY DID — 4:15–6:15 *(the differentiator)*

*[GRAPHIC D5: line chart — Temple median closed price 2019–2026, Meta announcement flagged at 2022 — "CTXMLS, ~31,000 listings, 16-year dataset · deemed reliable, not guaranteed"]*

Alright — the number I promised. I keep a sixteen-year MLS dataset for this market, about thirty-one thousand closed listings. Here's Temple's median closed price, year by year. 2019: $176,000. 2020: $194,000. 2021: $239,900. 2022 — the year Meta was announced — $290,000. Then 2023: $282,000. 2024: $279,500. 2025: $275,000. This year so far: about $268,000.

Read that again. Temple's median peaked the year Meta was ANNOUNCED — and it's drifted down about seven and a half percent since, through the entire construction of a $1.2 billion facility. Now, I can't hand that number to the protest side either, because you can't pin the decline on data centers — 2022 is when interest rates spiked and the whole country's market turned. Medians also shift with what's selling. That's exactly the point. The market followed rates and supply, like everywhere else in Texas. So when anyone — a builder rep, an agent, a guy on Facebook — promises you data centers are about to send Temple home values up, ask them why three billion dollars of announcements didn't move the line for four years. The honest answer: this hasn't been a home-price story. So far.

*[GRAPHIC D6: "Where the housing effect HAS shown up (inference, labeled): construction workforce → rental & mid-term-stay demand — 1,200 workers at peak"]*

Where does it show up? Rentals. Twelve hundred construction workers need somewhere to sleep, and they don't buy houses — they fill apartments, extended stays, and furnished mid-term rentals. I own a small furnished rental near the hospital myself, so I watch that demand channel with my own money — and that's the channel a construction boom actually moves. When one wave ends and the next project breaks ground, that demand rolls with it. If you own rental property here, that's your chapter of this story.

### CH 4 — THE CONCERNS, STRAIGHT — 6:15–8:00

*[GRAPHIC D7: "Water — per the development agreement: closed-loop · one-time fill 1–1.5M gal, recirculated 12–15 yrs · domestic use capped 4,000 gal/day · company avg claim ~350 gal/day" — every line source-tagged]*

Now the concerns — straight, no eye-rolling, because some of them are legitimate questions.

Water first, since it's the loudest one. The Rowan design is closed-loop: fill it once — one to one and a half million gallons — and recirculate for over a decade. The development agreement caps Project Temple's domestic water at four thousand gallons a day, and the company claims average use around 350 — about what one household runs. Those are the agreement's numbers and the company's numbers, not mine, and I'd tell you to read the agreement yourself — the city publishes a data-center FAQ. The honest version is: this design is nothing like the water-hungry data centers you've read about elsewhere, AND a contractual cap is only as good as its enforcement. Both true.

*[GRAPHIC D8: "Taxes — 10-yr abatement structure · city projects ~$7M/project during the decade, ~$12M/yr/project after — city/company projections, not guarantees"]*

Taxes. Yes, there's a ten-year abatement per project — that's real, and it's what a lot of the anger is about. The city's side of the ledger: it projects around seven million dollars per project in revenue even during the abatement decade, roughly doubling after. Those are projections, not guarantees, and I'm putting them on screen with that label. What I'll say as the guy who watches your property-tax bills: a data center consumes almost no schools, no parks, and no police compared to a subdivision of the same value. Whether the abatement was too generous is a fair political question — it's just not a housing question.

And the politics — you should know it's live. A recall effort against the mayor and two council members started in April over the Bob White annexation vote. The petition drive came up short for the November ballot; organizers say they're continuing toward the spring. The residents' stated concerns are water, infrastructure strain, and quality of life. The city's stated position is that annexing brought the projects under local zoning, utility protections, and revenue agreements. I'm not going to tell you how to feel about that — I'm telling you it exists, because the fourth project's hearings are happening right now, and what gets decided shapes the next one of these videos.

### CH 5 — SO SHOULD YOU BUY? — 8:00–9:30

*[GRAPHIC D9: "The corridor question: 1) Am I inside the direct zone? 2) Construction years vs operational years 3) What does the line say — not the story?"]*

So what do you actually do with this if you're buying?

If the home you're looking at is nowhere near these corridors — and statistically, it almost certainly isn't — this whole debate is a tax-base story for you, not a housing story. Buy on the normal things: payment, condition, commute, school zoning verified by address.

If it IS on or near a corridor — Bob White, the Industrial and Eberhardt side, out toward 3117 — now listen, here's where I'd slow you down. You're not buying today's street; you're buying years of construction and then a permanent industrial neighbor. Some buyers genuinely don't care and pick up the better deal — no restrictions on that side of town was already the tradeoff. Some buyers will care every single day. I'd rather lose you off a corridor lot now than list it for you in three years. And if you're being told corridor-adjacent land or houses carry a premium BECAUSE of the boom — you've seen the price line now. Make the seller prove it.

One more, for owners near the sites: you don't have to guess what this did to your specific street. The county's sales data and the MLS will show it, address by address. That's a twenty-minute exercise with real comps — not a Facebook thread.

## TAKE 5 — Warranty and the month-eleven move

**Target:** about 1 minute 10 seconds–1 minute 25 seconds  
**Cards:** `G7-warranty-timeline` → `G8-month-eleven`

> The warranty is where discipline matters.
>
> Stylecraft’s public warranty page confirms a one-year workmanship warranty. The project warranty materials describe a one-two-ten structure: one year for workmanship, two years for certain systems and ten years for qualifying structural coverage. The executed warranty, its exclusions and its claim procedure control, so read those documents rather than relying on a summary.
>
> My recommendation is not to wait until the anniversary date. Around month eleven, hire an independent inspector—typically around four to five hundred dollars, depending on the property and inspector. Ask for a written report, review every item against the warranty, and submit covered workmanship concerns through the required written process before Day three-sixty-five.
>
> Most Stylecraft inspections I have personally seen have been clean, and I am not promising that the inspection will uncover thousands of dollars in defects. The value is deadline protection. A clean report gives you a dated third-party snapshot of the home’s condition at that time. It does not guarantee that nothing will change in the future. If the report identifies a covered problem, you have documented it while the relevant warranty period may still be open.
>
> Save the report, submission, photographs, emails and responses. If a system or structural issue appears later, check the longer coverage section instead of assuming all warranty protection ended at year one.

## TAKE 6 — Hidden payment costs, taxes and district charges

**Target:** about 55–70 seconds  
**Card:** `G11-total-payment`; do not show a builder P&I example as the total payment

> The next trap is the difference between principal and interest and the actual monthly payment.
>
> The sixteen-ninety in Stylecraft’s official example is principal and interest only. Your real payment can also include property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, HOA dues and any applicable district assessments.
>
> New construction creates a common escrow surprise. Early tax records may reflect only the lot or a partially completed improvement. After the completed home is appraised, the taxable value can rise, and a later escrow analysis can raise the monthly payment. Do not build your budget around the first incomplete tax bill.
>
> Before signing, pull the current tax certificate or appraisal record for the exact lot, identify every taxing entity, and check for a MUD, PID or other special district. Then ask the lender to estimate the payment using a reasonable completed-home value—not merely the prior lot value.
>
> This is not tax advice, and exemptions vary. The practical rule is simple: underwrite the finished house, not the temporary first-year tax snapshot.

## TAKE 7 — The builder contract, inspection and walkthrough

**Target:** about 1 minute 15 seconds–1 minute 35 seconds  
**Card:** `G9-contract-review`

> Now the contract. A builder contract is not the same document most Texas buyers recognize from a resale transaction, and the exact version in front of you controls.
>
> Before you sign, locate five things in writing. First, what inspection, termination or option rights do you actually have? Do not assume the standard resale option period applies. Second, what rights does the builder have to substitute materials, finishes or plans? Third, is the closing date guaranteed, estimated or movable, and who carries the risk if a rate lock or lease expires? Fourth, what happens to your earnest money or other deposits if the transaction does not close? And fifth, which incentives disappear if you use a lender other than Kangaroo?
>
> Those are review questions, not a legal interpretation. If a provision is unclear or the money at risk is significant, ask a Texas real-estate attorney to explain it before you sign.
>
> The inspection sequence should also be written into your plan. Use an independent inspector before closing. Then attend the builder walkthrough and mark visible cosmetic or completion items. The blue-tape walkthrough is not a substitute for the inspection, and neither one guarantees that every requested item will be repaired.
>
> Put agreed repairs and unresolved items in writing, confirm what must be completed before closing, and preserve any post-closing obligation in the final documents. Do not rely on “we’ll take care of it” if the contract says something different.

## TAKE 8 — Who Stylecraft fits and who should keep shopping

**Target:** about 1 minute–1 minute 15 seconds  
**Card:** `G0-verdict-3-5` or verified Stylecraft plan/finish B-roll

> So who does Stylecraft fit?
>
> Stylecraft makes sense for a buyer whose main priorities are a competitive monthly payment, an accessible new-construction price point, practical standard features and a defined plan they can evaluate without major customization. It can also fit a buyer who is comfortable comparing the preferred-lender offer carefully and staying organized through inspection and warranty deadlines.
>
> It is probably not the right fit if you want major floor-plan changes, highly customized selections, a guaranteed move-in date, or premium craftsmanship regardless of price. It may also be a poor fit if you do not want to document and follow up on warranty items, or if using an affiliated preferred lender is a deal breaker for you.
>
> Once the price moves into the low four-hundreds, widen the comparison. At that level, payment is still important, but so are lot, location, finish level, service history and resale competition. A lower rate does not automatically make the higher-priced house the better purchase.
>
> The right question is not, “Is Stylecraft good or bad?” It is, “Does this exact Stylecraft home beat the other homes available for my budget, timeline and total monthly cost?”

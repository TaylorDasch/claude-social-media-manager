One more thing on this, because it's the most common thing I see. Builder sales reps will sometimes offer a discount or incentive that they say disappears if you bring an agent. I want to be direct with you: under TREC rules, a builder structuring buyer-side incentives conditional on the buyer being unrepresented is a steering signal. It's not a deal. It's a flag. If a builder is structuring incentives that way, ask yourself what else in the contract is structured to favor them.

Are some unbundled builder offers legitimate? Yes. Title-company credits, builder-preferred lender rate buy-downs — those exist and they can be real. But they're separate from your representation. You can take a builder's lender if their rate is genuinely better. You're not required to. I'll show you how to compare in a minute.

**[5:45–6:30 — TREC FORM 1502]**

Quick on the form itself. TREC Form 1502 is the buyer representation agreement promulgated by the Texas Real Estate Commission. There's an exclusive version and a non-exclusive version. For a BSW resident who hasn't picked an agent yet, the non-exclusive version is the lower-friction option — it lets you work with a specific agent on a specific property or set of properties without committing to one agent for everything. No fee to you. The builder pays buyer-agent commission at closing the same way any seller does. The form is publicly available on the TREC website. You sign it digitally in about three minutes. That's the gate.

Builder-preferred lenders sometimes offer rate buy-downs that genuinely beat the market — I'll help you run the comparison. The number to compare is APR after all incentives, not the headline rate.

The non-negotiable disclosure here: no lender is required. The builder cannot make you use their lender. If they tell you the price changes based on lender, that's a red flag — and that's where the legal framework I'm about to walk through comes in.

**[8:00–9:00 — LEGAL CATEGORY CORRECTION]**

Quick legal aside, because this is where BSW residents get the worst advice. There are four real frameworks that govern what builders and lenders can and cannot do.

- **RESPA Section 8** — the federal Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act. It prohibits kickbacks between builders and lenders for steering buyers.
- **TREC Section 535.148** — the Texas Real Estate Commission rule on broker conduct and undisclosed agency.
- **TREC Section 535.155** — advertising and disclosure requirements.
- **Texas DTPA Section 17.46** — the Deceptive Trade Practices Act, false or misleading practices.

If a builder lies about whether you can be represented, or about whether the price changes with a different lender, those are the laws that apply.

Now the correction, because I hear this from residents constantly. Some people will tell you that **Stark Law** restricts you as a physician or resident from getting buyer representation or receiving certain incentives. That is wrong. Stark Law — 42 USC 1395nn — is a Medicare and Medicaid self-referral statute for healthcare services. It governs what physicians can refer to entities they have a financial interest in within the healthcare system. It has zero application to real estate brokerage, or to a builder paying your buyer's agent commission at closing. If anyone — a sales rep, a colleague, a hospital finance officer — tells you Stark Law applies to your home purchase, they are wrong on the law. You can repeat that. You should repeat that.

I'm not your attorney. None of this is legal advice. I'm naming the actual frameworks. Stark Law is not one of them.

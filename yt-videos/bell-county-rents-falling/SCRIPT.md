*[Shot: screen recording, scrolling the lease export]*

Let me show you where this comes from, because it's the part that matters.

Most rent numbers you've seen for Central Texas are model estimates. Zillow, RentCast,

This is different. This is the actual MLS lease record. Two thousand seventy-four
leases, and sixteen hundred twenty-four of those have a recorded close price — meaning
somebody actually signed at that number. Not asked. **Signed.**

*[LOWER THIRD: 2,074 lease records · 1,624 executed]*

And I do one thing with this data that almost nobody does, which changes the number
more than you'd think.

I never blend single-family houses with duplex and apartment units.

*[Shot: side-by-side on screen — house photo vs duplex photo]*

A three-bedroom house in Temple rents at sixteen ninety-five. A three-bedroom duplex
unit? Thirteen ninety-five. Three hundred dollars apart, every month.

*[LOWER THIRD: House $1,695 · Duplex unit $1,395 · same 3 bed]*

When a portal blends those into one median, it understates house rents by about nine
percent. If you comped your rent off a portal estimate, that's your first error and you
don't know it's there.

## THE DIRECTION (3:40–5:10)
*[Shot: the trend chart. Let it breathe on screen.]*

A median tells you where rents are. It doesn't tell you where they're going.

June of last year, county-wide, three-bedroom single-family median was eighteen hundred
dollars. June this year? Seventeen hundred.

*[LOWER THIRD: $1,800 → $1,700 · −5.6%]*

That's down five point six percent. Temple's off five point three. Killeen — and pay
attention if you own near Fort Hood — Killeen is down eight point six percent.

*[LOWER THIRD: Killeen −8.6% YoY]*

Now I want to be careful here, because this is where people overreact. This is twelve
months of data. It is not a crash, it is not a collapse, and I'm not telling you Central
Texas is broken. Rents softening after the run-up we had is a normal thing for a market
to do.

But it is absolutely not three percent growth. And that's what's sitting in your
spreadsheet right now.

**So change one: underwrite flat.** Not down. Flat. Make rent growth your upside case,
not the thing the deal depends on. If it only pencils in year two when rent goes up,
you don't have a deal — you have a bet.

## VACANCY IS MEASURABLE (5:10–6:45)
*[Shot: Taylor to camera, then the lease-up table]*

Second change, and this one's my favorite because almost nobody does it.

Everybody plugs in eight percent vacancy. Where'd that come from? A podcast. A template.
Somebody's rule of thumb.

You don't have to guess. Every lease record carries how many days that property sat on
market before somebody signed.

Temple: thirty-one days, median.
Belton: thirty-seven.
Killeen: forty-eight.

*[LOWER THIRD: Median days to lease — Temple 31 · Belton 37 · Killeen 48]*

But the median isn't what you underwrite. Look at the slow quarter. In Temple, one in
four takes seventy days or more. In Killeen, eighty-eight.

Run that out. A Temple house on an annual turnover is about seven point eight percent
vacancy drag — so eight percent is roughly right for Temple, fine. But that same math
in Killeen? Eleven point six percent.

*[LOWER THIRD: Killeen annual-turnover vacancy ≈ 11.6% vs 8% assumed]*

You just found three and a half points of expense that wasn't in your model. On a
fifteen-hundred-dollar rent, that's six hundred dollars a year you were about to not
account for. Every year you own it.

**Change two: pull your vacancy number off the lease-up data for the specific city
you're buying in.** Not the county. Not a template. The city.

## NEW VS OLD (6:45–8:00)
*[Shot: B-roll — new construction street, then older established street]*

Third one, and this is the one that surprised me.

The instinct with a rental is buy the cheaper older house. Lower entry price, better
yield on paper.

Temple homes built 2020 or later lease at eighteen fifty. Pre-2000 stock? Fourteen
hundred.

*[LOWER THIRD: Built 2020+ $1,850 · Pre-2000 $1,400 · +32%]*

That's four hundred fifty dollars a month. Thirty-two percent. And that's not a
small-sample fluke — that's a hundred three newer leases against eighty-five older ones.
Killeen shows the same shape, thirty-five percent.

So the older house is cheaper to buy. It is not cheaper to own, and it does not rent
anywhere near the same. Before you write off new construction because the sticker's
higher, run the rent side. Sometimes the builder incentive plus the rent premium beats
the older house on cash flow, and sometimes it doesn't — but you can't know that if
you're only looking at purchase price.

**Change three: price the rent line by build era, not by square footage alone.**

## Chapter 1 · 0:28–1:55 · The 28/36 Rule (what you can actually afford)

**Chapter title card:** "01 · How much house can you actually afford?"

**[SHOT: Taylor at desk with iPad/whiteboard. Numbers written cleanly. Two columns visible.]**

> Lenders will approve you for more than you should spend. That's not a conspiracy — it's just how their underwriting models work. They're approving the maximum you can technically carry, not the comfortable monthly payment that leaves you with a life.
>
> The rule that survives every market cycle: **housing payment under 28% of your gross monthly income. Total debt under 36%.** That's the 28/36 rule.

**[ON-SCREEN GRAPHIC: 28/36 callout. Big numbers. Emerald accent. Build the math live.]**

> Run the math with me. Household earning $7,000 a month gross — that's about $84K a year, before taxes. Your target maximum PITI — that's principal, interest, taxes, insurance — is roughly $1,960 a month. At today's rates, that maps to about a $260K to $280K purchase price.
>
> Here's the line that matters: if your housing payment hits 32% of your gross income, you're house-poor before you move in. The walls are nicer. The bank account is empty. Every HVAC failure becomes a crisis.

**[SHOT: Cut back to Taylor on-camera, leaning in slightly.]**

> The honest version of pre-approval isn't "what's my max?" It's "what payment doesn't strain my life?" Tell your lender that, in those words. Most won't lead with it. Force the conversation.

## Chapter 2 · 1:55–3:40 · The 5-loan comparison (with a verdict)

**Chapter title card:** "02 · FHA, VA, Conventional, USDA, or TSAHC — which one is actually yours?"

**[SHOT: Taylor on-camera, then cut to clean comparison graphic. Five loan cards, one at a time.]**

> Most first-time buyers default to FHA without running the alternatives. That's the single most common mistake I see at the lender stage. Here are the five options. Match the loan to your profile.

**[GRAPHIC: 5-card lower-third sequence as Taylor narrates each one.]**

> **One. FHA.** Three and a half percent down. Credit floor of 580 — or 500 to 579 with 10% down. Forgiving on debt-to-income. The catch: mortgage insurance for the life of the loan unless you refinance. Best when down payment savings are thin or credit is rebuilding.
>
> **Two. VA.** Zero down. No PMI. Available to active-duty, veterans, and qualifying spouses. Funding fee waived for service-connected disability. Here's a feature most people miss — VA loans are assumable by another VA-eligible buyer. That can be a hidden selling feature five years from now.
>
> **Three. Conventional.** Best for higher-credit buyers — 740 and up — who want to avoid FHA's permanent mortgage insurance. First-time buyer programs like HomeReady and Home Possible allow 3% down. PMI drops automatically at 78% loan-to-value.
>
> **Four. USDA.** Zero down. Income-capped. Covers most of Bell County outside the Temple, Belton, and Killeen city cores — Salado, parts of Troy, Nolanville fringe, Holland, rural Bell. If your target zip is rural, USDA is often cheaper than FHA over the life of the loan.
>
> **Five. TSAHC stacked with MCC.** The one most first-time buyers don't know exists. We'll spend a full chapter on this in a minute.

**[SHOT: Taylor on-camera, verdict frame.]**

> Here's the decisive line. If you qualify for VA, you take VA — every time. If you qualify for USDA in a rural Bell County zip, you take USDA. FHA is the fallback, not the default. Conventional is the move when your credit is strong and you want PMI to actually disappear someday.
>
> The right lender will model all four against your specific profile before you tour a single home. If they don't, you have the wrong lender.

**[B-ROLL: Phone screen showing four side-by-side rate quotes. Anonymized.]**

## Chapter 4 · 5:05–6:30 · TSAHC + MCC — the $2,000/year nobody told you about

**Chapter title card:** "04 · The grant + tax credit stack most lenders won't mention"

**[SHOT: Taylor on-camera, slightly forward lean — this is the "you need to hear this" chapter.]**

> Here's the chapter that's going to upset some lenders. Most first-time buyers in Bell County don't know these programs exist. The ones who do leave $5,000 to $10,000 on the table by not stacking them.

**[GRAPHIC: Stack diagram building bottom-up — Base Loan → TSAHC Grant → MCC.]**

> Layer one: **TSAHC.** Texas State Affordable Housing Corporation. Two programs to know — Home Sweet Texas Home Loan, and Homes for Texas Heroes for teachers, nurses, peace officers, EMTs, firefighters, corrections officers, and veterans. Both give you a **3% to 5% down payment grant**. Not a loan. A grant. Never repaid. Income cap roughly $92K to $108K for Bell County depending on household size. Most Bell County zips qualify.
>
> Layer two: **MCC. Mortgage Credit Certificate.** Federal income tax credit worth up to 30% to 40% of your mortgage interest paid annually, capped at **$2,000 a year, for the life of the loan**, as long as the home is your primary residence. On a $280K loan at 7% APR, your first-year credit lands somewhere between $1,800 and $2,000.

**[SHOT: Taylor on-camera, simple frame.]**

> Now stack them. TSAHC grant covers most or all of your down payment. MCC drops your effective monthly payment by about $150 to $165 a month for as long as you own the home. Some Bell County first-time buyers close on a $280K home with **under $3,000 out of pocket** when this stack works.

**[B-ROLL: Hand on screen showing TSAHC homepage, then MCC fact sheet. No specific lender logos.]**

> The catch — and this is the part lenders don't always volunteer — **not every lender writes these programs.** The per-loan margin is thinner. Some of the biggest national lenders simply don't bother. If you ask your lender about TSAHC and they say "we don't do those," that's your answer. Call a second lender who does. Verify current 2026 program details at tsahc.org.

## Chapter 5 · 6:30–8:05 · The seven traps (with the builder upgrade trap leading)

**Chapter title card:** "05 · The seven traps that crush first-time buyers in Temple"

**[SHOT: Taylor on-camera, brisk pace. This is the longest chapter — keep the tempo up.]**

> Seven traps. Some you've heard of. Most first-time buyer content sanitizes the rest. I'm not.

**[GRAPHIC: Seven numbered amber-accent cards build under VO as Taylor narrates each.]**

> **Trap one. The builder upgrade trap.** This is the most expensive mistake on the list. You walk into a $300K model home. You sign for $385K. Why? Because the model is loaded with $60K to $120K in options. Level-five granite. Upgraded carpet pad. The smart-home package. Trim level three. Most of those upgrades don't recoup at resale.
>
> The rule: **stick to structural upgrades only.** Lot premium. Square footage. Garage size. Skip everything else. Bring an agent who has negotiated with that specific builder.

**[B-ROLL: Walk-through of a model home, focal on a "design center" upgrade brochure, then a quick visual of "base price" vs "signed price" overlay.]**

> **Trap two. Foundation issues in older Temple and Belton homes.** Central Texas clay soil. Pre-2000 homes commonly show post-tension or pier-and-beam foundation movement. **Get a structural engineer inspection on any pre-2000 home.** Separate from the standard home inspection. Four hundred to seven hundred dollars. Worth every dollar.

**[B-ROLL: Close-up of a hairline foundation crack, then a structural report.]**

> **Trap three. MUD or PID tax surprise.** Several master-planned communities in Belton, Lake Belton, and Salado carry Municipal Utility District or Public Improvement District taxes. That adds $1,500 to $4,000 a year on top of your normal property tax. **Pull the tax detail before you offer.**

> **Trap four. HOA fee creep plus future special assessments.** HOAs in master-planned Belton communities often start around $400 a year and creep to $900 within five years. Some have hit owners with $3,000 to $5,000 special assessments for pool repairs or amenity rebuilds. **Read the last three years of HOA board minutes before closing.** They're public. Ask for them.

**[B-ROLL: Stack of HOA documents fanned out.]**

> **Trap five. The Texas insurance crisis.** Premiums up 30% to 60% in many zones since 2022. Some carriers have exited the state. **Get insurance quotes during the option period — not after.** A surprise premium can blow up your debt-to-income ratio and kill the loan three days before closing. I've watched it happen.

> **Trap six. Closing the same day you start a new job.** Lenders re-verify employment inside the last 10 days before closing. **Don't change jobs in the 60 days before close.** Don't quit. Don't switch from W-2 to 1099. Don't get a weird bonus deposited. The underwriter is watching.

> **Trap seven. Buying without a buyer's agent.** In most Texas transactions, the seller pays both commissions — meaning **buying represented costs you nothing extra.** The sales agent at the new construction model is the builder's agent, not yours. The listing agent on a resale is the seller's agent, not yours. Walking unrepresented is the most expensive "save" first-time buyers make.

## Chapter 6 · 8:05–9:00 · The 90-day timeline

**Chapter title card:** "06 · From cold-start to keys-in-hand in 60 to 90 days"

**[SHOT: Taylor on-camera, then cut to clean six-step visual timeline.]**

> Final piece. The order of operations. A clean Temple first-time buyer transaction runs 60 to 90 days. The pre-approval is the bottleneck — start it before you tour a single home.

**[GRAPHIC: Six-step vertical timeline builds as Taylor reads each step.]**

> **Days 1 to 7.** Pull credit. Dispute errors — that takes two to three weeks, so start now. Gather W-2s, two months of pay stubs, two months of bank statements, two years of tax returns. Submit to a lender. Get the pre-approval letter pegged to a comfortable payment, not your max ceiling.
>
> **Days 8 to 14.** Pick the loan. Pick the agent. Run the comparison on all five loan types. Pick an agent with documented experience in your target neighborhoods.
>
> **Weeks 3 to 6.** Tour 6 to 12 homes. Tour both new construction and resale so you see the real tradeoffs. Make the offer with a 7 to 10 day option period. Earnest money typically $1K to $2K in Bell County.
>
> **Option period — days 7 to 14.** General inspection, $400 to $600. Structural engineer if it's a pre-2000 home, $500 to $800. Insurance quotes inside the option period. Not after.
>
> **Weeks 7 to 11.** Underwriting and appraisal. Lender orders the appraisal, $600 to $700. Don't quit your job. Don't open new credit. Don't buy furniture on credit until after closing.
>
> **Days 60 to 90.** Final walk-through 24 hours before close. Sign at the title company. Wire the closing funds. Receive keys.

**[SHOT: Taylor on-camera, final beat of the chapter.]**

> One thing most first-time buyers miss — **file your homestead exemption with Bell County Appraisal District before April 30 of the year following your purchase.** Saves you real money on property taxes. Most people don't, and pay full tax for an extra year.

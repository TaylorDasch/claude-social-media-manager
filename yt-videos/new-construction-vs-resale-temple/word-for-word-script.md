## 2:35-4:20 - The Tax Line Buyers Miss

**VISUAL:** Tax card. `Source: templetxhomes.net`.

**SAY:**

The big one is district taxes and assessments.

Some newer Bell County and Temple-area developments sit inside a MUD or a PID.

That means Municipal Utility District or Public Improvement District.

Plain English: it is an extra assessment that can help pay for roads, water lines, and infrastructure around the development.

And it can land on your tax bill.

This is not a theory from some other part of Texas.

Bell County has named districts on the books.

Bell County MUD Number One, for example, covers the Three Creeks community over in Belton.

That does not mean every new build has one.

It does not mean every Temple address has one.

It means you verify the specific address before you compare payments.

Here is why it matters.

Take a two hundred eighty thousand dollar home.

At a normal Temple property tax rate around two point three seven percent, that is about six thousand six hundred thirty-six dollars a year.

Now use a three thousand two hundred dollar district-charge example.

That pushes the annual number to about nine thousand eight hundred thirty-six dollars.

That is about two hundred sixty-seven dollars a month you may not have noticed in the listing price.

Over a thirty-year mortgage, that can stack toward six figures.

That is enough to wipe out a big chunk of a builder incentive.

So before you choose a floor plan, ask this in writing:

"Is this exact address inside a MUD or a PID, and what is the current rate?"

Do not assume yes.

Do not assume no.

Verify the parcel.

## 6:20-7:35 - The Model Home Trap

**VISUAL:** Staged sign-in card. Write: `Yes - Taylor Dasch, EG Realty`.

**SAY:**

Now here is where buyers give away leverage without meaning to.

They walk into the model home alone.

The builder sales rep is not there to represent you.

That person represents the builder's side of the deal.

That does not make them bad.

That is just who they work for.

So before you tour, get your own agent involved and make sure your representation is documented before you register with the builder.

In many builder deals, if your agent is registered before you tour, the builder has already budgeted buyer-agent compensation.

So it typically costs you nothing out of pocket.

Confirm that in writing.

Walking in alone usually does not save you money.

It usually just removes the person whose job is to help you compare the builder offer against the resale options, the tax bill, the inspection risk, and the monthly payment.

Here is the simple decision framework.

Choose new if you want lower early maintenance, a more predictable process, and a modern layout.

Choose new if your timeline can handle the builder process.

Choose new if the incentive math still works after you include taxes, HOA, insurance, lender fees, and what happens when the rate changes.

But there are two gates I would not skip.

Gate one: your budget needs to work after any MUD or PID cost.

Gate two: the incentive still needs to make sense after the preferred-lender terms and long-term payment are compared against resale.

Those two gates are pass or fail.

If either one fails, new construction may not be your move, no matter how good the flyer looks.

Choose resale if you want a more established street, a mature lot, or a shorter closing timeline.

Choose resale if you want more freedom to shop lenders.

Choose resale if you want to avoid district-tax risk on the specific address.

Choose resale if you are comfortable budgeting for repairs based on the inspection.

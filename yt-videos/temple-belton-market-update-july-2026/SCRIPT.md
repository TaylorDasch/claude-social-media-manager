## 2:52–3:40 — Why a price cut is not a second discount

[CARD: `TEACHING EXAMPLE — NOT A REAL LISTING`.]

Imagine a home starts at $320,000, sits, and gets reduced to $304,900. If it closes around $303,500, the buyer is roughly $16,500 below the original ask—but only $1,400 below the corrected ask.

The portal shows a red price-cut arrow. The actual negotiation says the seller already did most of the moving in public.

So at day 90, my next question is not, “How big should the lowball be?”

It is, “Why is this still here after the cut?” Is it condition, location, insurance, title, layout, taxes, seller constraints, or simply a thin buyer pool at this price?

A prior reduction is evidence that a correction happened. It is not proof that another large correction is available.

But the 31 homes in that same price band that closed in the latest window had a median 37 days on market and a median close-to-original-list ratio of 99.73%.

That is not a contradiction. Stale leftovers and correctly positioned winners can occupy the same price band.

It is also why I would not use Temple’s citywide Active median of 69 days—or Belton’s 73 and a half—to dictate an offer on one house.

## 5:24–6:51 — The three-number offer test

[RETURN TO THREE BLANK BOXES; FILL THEM ONE AT A TIME.]

So here is the actual test I would use on the house in your saved list.

### Number one: relative days on market

Compare its DOM with the same city, price band, property type, condition, and closest reasonable alternatives—not only the citywide median.

The 60 and 90-day marks tell me when to investigate. They do not tell me the answer.

### Number two: current price versus original price

Has the seller already moved $5,000, $20,000, or not at all? How recently? Did the new price put the home beside better choices?

Remember the latest closing relationship: 97.01% of original list, but 99.76% of final list. Price history can carry more offer signal than the badge beside the listing.

### Number three: substitute pace

What happened to the closest similar homes? Did they sit, reduce, go pending quickly, withdraw, or close near the final ask? Does the buyer have three true alternatives, or none?

[GRAPHIC: `RELATIVE DOM + PRICE HISTORY + SUBSTITUTE PACE`.]

I want all three pointing the same direction before I recommend a more aggressive price or term conversation.

Long relative DOM, little prior correction, and slow substitutes can support a harder price or term discussion.

Fresh exposure, meaningful prior correction, and fast substitutes can mean the buyer needs to decide quickly.

That still does not tell you what a seller will accept. It does not replace property-specific comps, condition, inspection, insurance, title, or financing review. It tells you which conversation the evidence supports.

## 6:51–7:56 — Buy now or wait?

[DECISION TREE: `FIXED MOVE DATE` / `FLEXIBLE MOVE DATE`.]

Now turn that into the relocator’s real question: should I buy now or wait?

If your move date is fixed, your monthly cost is comfortable, and the right home is supported by its comps, this data gives you no reason to freeze because of a broad headline. You have visible correction and real choice—but a fresh, well-positioned home can still move.

If your timing is flexible, waiting can make sense when the payment feels stretched, your job or move date is uncertain, or the available homes do not fit.

Waiting because you know Temple and Belton are about to crash is not a conclusion this data supports. Through July 17, combined sales were down only 0.9% from the same part of 2025. The median closed price was down 1.4%, and median DOM was three days longer.

[FOOTNOTE: `Based on information from Central Texas MLS for Jan. 1–July 17, 2025 and Jan. 1–July 17, 2026. Temple + Belton Closed records at or above $25,000; deduplicated; medians; seller credits unavailable.`]

For an out-of-town buyer, I would have financing ready, keep a ranked backup list, and reserve the trip for homes that pass the three-number test **and** the ownership-cost checks.

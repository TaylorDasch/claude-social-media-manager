**[1:00 — THE MECHANICS, NOT THE MOTIVE]**

And before you think this is just a Belton story — Temple has the most new‑construction inventory in the region: three hundred eighty‑one homes at our last pull. These builders are competing for *your* buyer too.

So let's start with why those incentives exist — because if you read it wrong, you'll either panic or get cocky, and both cost you money.

A finished spec home carries the builder's interest, taxes, and insurance every single day it sits. That's the rational reason for the incentive stack — not distress. When you see a price cut and a rate buydown stacked together, you're not looking at a desperate company; you're looking at a balance‑sheet decision a national builder is structured to make, and you, as one homeowner, are not.

Here's the proof of how long that capital sits. Our published market analysis put new‑construction days on market at a hundred and six, against sixty for resale — a forty‑six‑day gap. And on the live board, you can see individual spec homes sitting two hundred fifty, three hundred, even four hundred days. One off Pima Lane is at four hundred days right now.

So read each incentive line as a signal — rate buydowns, closing‑cost credits, price reductions, preferred‑lender requirements. Each one tells you how long a builder has been carrying that home. That's not a reason to feel small. It's the first piece of your leverage. Because you'll never win a price war against a company that builds its discounts into the spreadsheet — so stop fighting on price, and start fighting on the three things the spreadsheet can't address.

**[2:15 — LEVER 1: NO DISTRICT (MUD/PID) DRAG]**

Lever one. And this is the one nobody tells the seller about.

A builder's discount is a one‑time thing the buyer spends once. But on some of those homes, there's a cost the buyer pays every month — for thirty years.

Some newer Bell County developments sit inside a special taxing district — a MUD or a PID. Bell County Municipal Utility District Number One, at Three Creeks in Belton, is one example. From our published market analysis, those districts can carry two to five thousand dollars a year in extra tax. On a roughly two‑hundred‑eighty‑thousand‑dollar home, that's about two hundred sixty‑seven dollars a month — stacking toward around ninety‑six thousand dollars over the life of a thirty‑year loan. Enough, in that illustration, to quietly erase the builder's entire incentive package.

Now — hard rule, say it with me: this only applies if a home is actually inside one of those districts. Many resale homes aren't. And not every new build is, either. So you verify the specific property — yours and any home you're being compared to — and you never assume. Don't guess on this; checking your specific district status is the first thing I do in the free review.

But here's the seller move: if your home is outside a MUD or a PID, that is a real, quantifiable advantage. And it doesn't belong buried — it belongs in your listing.

**[3:45 — LEVER 2: NO PREFERRED‑LENDER LOCK]**

Lever two. Look back at that live board.

Those big rate buydowns and closing credits? On the deepest‑incentive homes — the Pecan Meadow home with the seventy‑nine‑thousand‑dollar cut, the Liberty Park home, the Red Fish Court home — the incentive is tied to using the builder's preferred lender. That's a real, common condition on the board.

I'm not knocking anyone's lender. The buydown is real money. But it comes with the builder's lender and the builder's terms. A buyer purchasing your resale home keeps the freedom to shop every lender in town — compare every rate and every fee, which may or may not beat the builder's buydown. That's the buyer's call to make. With your home, they get to make it.

So your positioning isn't "I'm cheaper than new." It's "no financing lock‑in." That's a benefit the buyer feels at the closing table — and it costs you nothing to offer.

**[5:00 — LEVER 3: FASTER TYPICAL CLOSE]**

Lever three. Here's why it matters before I hedge it: on a relocation timeline — a new job start date, a lease running out — a buyer can actually plan their move around a resale close. They can't reliably plan around a build that may slip.

Our published May analysis compared resale homes at a median of sixty days on market to new construction at a hundred and six. So when I say a well‑positioned resale home tends to move faster, I'm talking about *that* published sixty‑day resale figure — not a promise *your* home hits it. And let me separate the numbers so nothing looks crossed: that sixty‑versus‑one‑oh‑six is resale‑versus‑new from our published analysis; today's overall market median, across every home type, is seventy‑one days. Different cut, different number — both true.

"Typically" is the operative word. Any individual sale depends on price, condition, and buyer demand — outcomes vary, and days on market is market‑dependent. Nothing here is a promise.

**[6:10 — HONEST NEGATIVE]**

Let me hold myself to the same standard. None of this works if your price is wrong. An overpriced resale home will sit there with no district tax, a free lender choice, and still not move. The levers amplify a right price; they can't rescue a wrong one.

And new construction is *not* "not worth it." Our published position is neutral — it depends. Some buyers happily pay the premium for new, for the warranty, for the rate buydown. Your job as a seller isn't to trash new builds. It's to make the total‑cost case, cleanly, to the buyer for whom it actually matters.

**[6:40 — THE PLAYBOOK]**

So here's how you use all of this when you list.

One — price to your strengths, not against their sticker. Our published analysis put new construction at roughly a hundred seventy‑four dollars a square foot against a hundred forty‑nine for resale — about a seventeen‑percent premium *per square foot* to buy new. That premium is your pricing room. You're not selling a cheaper version of the same house — you're selling a different total‑cost package.

Two — put your invisible advantages in the listing. If you're outside a MUD or PID, say so. "Finance with any lender." A finished yard and established trees the buyer doesn't pay to add. Things a brand‑new spec on a bare lot can't claim.

Three — have your agent build you a side‑by‑side total‑cost sheet against the specific discounted new builds the buyer is actually weighing. Not vibes — numbers.

Four — get a net sheet and a competitive‑position review before you ever pick a number. You frame the close like this: you're not the cheap option. You're the option without the district drag, without the lender lock, that closes on a timeline the buyer can plan around.

**[8:00 — CTA / SOFT CLOSE]** *(~40s)*

If you want that done for your specific home, that's exactly what I do — free, no obligation: your district‑tax status, your real comps, a net sheet, and the live builder board for your submarket, so you can see where your home stands before you price.

Now the part nobody wants to say out loud. Finance that at the median. The long-term thesis is real, but only if you control your basis below median, or you step up to the play that actually pays for the proximity.

**Play two — the mid-term rental for traveling clinicians. In this pocket, this is the yield angle I'd test first — and I want to show you exactly where it does and doesn't pay.**
Buy below median, around a hundred ten, put forty into rehab, then furnish it — about ten thousand more. Call it a hundred sixty thousand all-in including furniture. Furnished mid-term rent to a traveling clinician, verify-day-of, is researched at roughly fourteen hundred to eighteen hundred a month — and I want to be honest that the top of that band is the part I have not confirmed. So I'll underwrite the middle, around fifteen-fifty, not the top.
Here's the catch most people miss: furnished is expensive to run. You pay all the utilities and internet, around two thousand four hundred a year. Specialized mid-term management runs twelve to fifteen percent, not eight to ten. You carry gap-vacancy between contracts, cleaning and turns, and furniture that depreciates. Stack that and your expense load is in the mid-seventies percent — heavier than the long-term rental, not lighter. At fifteen-fifty a month, that's about forty-five hundred in NOI on a hundred-sixty-thousand all-in — a yield-on-cost of about two-point-nine percent.
Now compare that honestly, like for like, same below-median basis: the long-term rental I just showed you was about three-point-four. So at the mid of the band, the furnished play actually trails the plain long-term rental, because the rent premium gets eaten by the furnished operating load. The MTR edge only shows up near the top of the band — at eighteen hundred a month, if you can actually get it, you're around four-point-two percent yield-on-cost and now you're ahead. So this is the play I'd test first, but the whole thesis rests on verifying you can clear the upper band. Confirm the furnished rent before you furnish anything. The three-to-four-minute drive to the hospital is what gives you a shot at that upper band — nobody pays a furnished premium to live twenty minutes out — but proximity is the reason to test it, not proof that it pays.

**Play three — value-add flip, and this is where the real money in this neighborhood actually lives.**
Here's the thing the yield math above quietly understates: in a value-add play, your return doesn't come from year-one rent — it comes from the equity you create on day one. So judge it on that, not on cap rate. Comps support an after-repair value around one-eighty to two-ten; I'll underwrite to a hundred ninety. On South Seventh: eleven-oh-one sold at a hundred twenty thousand, ten-oh-eight square feet, built 1928. Twelve-oh-two at a hundred thirty-nine. Eighteen-fourteen — eight-forty square feet, 1955 — at a hundred fifty-seven; that's one of mine, so I know that number firsthand. But eleven-oh-two — thirteen-forty-four square feet, 1965 — sold at a hundred ninety-two. Newer and bigger wins; your exit is square footage and finish, not the dirt.
If your exit is a sale, run the seventy-percent rule hard. On a one-ninety after-repair value with forty in rehab, seventy percent is a hundred thirty-three, minus the forty puts your maximum buy near ninety-three thousand — below the median. That's the discipline: in a ninety-seven-percent sale-to-list market your exit buyer has leverage too, so the flip-to-sell only pencils if you buy genuinely cheap and the post-rehab square footage carries the comp.

**Play three-and-a-half — and this is the one I'd actually run: value-add to hold, the forced-equity play.**
Don't flip it — keep it. Say you buy at a hundred twenty, put forty in, you're all-in a hundred sixty against an after-repair value of one-ninety. You just created about thirty thousand dollars of equity — roughly nineteen percent of your all-in — on day one, by fixing the thing nobody else would. That's the return. Then it compounds: you season it and refinance. At seventy-five percent of a one-ninety value, you can pull roughly a hundred forty-two thousand back out, which leaves only around seventeen, eighteen thousand of your own cash in the deal, and you keep the equity. From there the tenant — backed by that twelve-thousand-person employer — pays down your loan, and you hold for long-run appreciation off that demand floor. So the honest answer to "where's the return if the yield is thin?" is: it's the equity you force, the principal someone else pays down, and time. Not a fat first-year check. This is a forced-equity and hold play for a cash or low-leverage operator who can execute a rehab — it is not a finance-at-the-median-and-collect-cash-flow play, and anyone selling you the second version is selling you the negative number I showed you earlier.

**Play four — small multifamily.**
About eight percent of today's sample — nine of roughly a hundred thirteen — is zoned commercial, multi-family, or mixed-use; three-oh-nine South Thirty-First is listed multi-family zoned. If you want two doors on one foundation next to a hospital, this is one of the few Temple submarkets where the zoning cooperates — verify the specific parcel before you underwrite it.

**[5:25–6:35 — THE FOUNDATION RISK = THE MOAT]**
Now the part I refuse to soft-pedal, because if I do, someone buys a money pit on my word.
These homes are pier-and-beam on Bell County black clay — expansive clay that swells when it's wet, shrinks when it's dry, and moves the foundation with it. In today's sample, about four percent of listings flag foundation issues outright — five name it directly — and the real number is almost certainly higher, because foundation problems don't always show up in the remarks. Absence of the word "foundation" in a listing is not clearance. Roughly a third — thirty-seven of a hundred thirteen — mention as-is, rehab, repair, or foundation. That pattern is the neighborhood telling you what it is.
So budget for it: six thousand dollars minimum for foundation work, range five to fifteen depending on severity. And do not lean on a general home inspector for this — hire a structural engineer. Different scope, different liability, different number. Non-negotiable line item.
And it's not just the slab. Pre-1960 stock means galvanized plumbing rusting shut, knob-and-tube or outdated wiring, asbestos and lead in anything pre-1978. That's why the rehab to a livable rental standard is thirty to fifty thousand, not ten.
Here's the reframe, and it's the whole game. I told you the rental demand is already priced in — it is. You're not going to out-discover the market on demand; the investors already know about the hospital. So where's your actual edge? It's not information. It's execution. Almost nobody can correctly underwrite the condition risk — the foundation, the clay, the full rehab on hundred-year-old systems — and fewer still can execute it. The clay is the problem you have to price correctly. I won't pretend otherwise. The discount exists because that problem is real and most people won't touch it. Your edge isn't a secret listing — there isn't one. Your edge is being the operator who can price and fix the hard part, force the equity, and hold, when retail buyers and weekend flippers can't. That friction is what keeps the median sub-one-fifty next to a twelve-thousand-person employer. You don't want this easy. You want to be the one who can do the hard part.

Who should avoid it? Anyone financing at the median and expecting cash flow — you'll be writing checks, seven to eight hundred a month of them. Anyone who wants passive and hands-off on day one. Anyone underwriting on rents they haven't verified this week, especially the top of the MTR band. And anyone who hears "pier-and-beam on clay" and doesn't budget for it. If you need it pretty or easy, this neighborhood will punish you.

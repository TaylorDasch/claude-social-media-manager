**Play one — buy-and-hold, long-term rental. And I'll be honest: at the median, this is the thinnest play here.**
Underwrite it at the actual number this video is built on. Buy at the closed median — a hundred forty-nine, nine ninety-nine — plus a rehab to a livable rental standard. Budget thirty to fifty thousand; I'll use forty. That's about a hundred ninety thousand all-in.

On pre-1960 stock the expense load is real: taxes, insurance, vacancy, repairs, management. Underwrite around forty-five percent. That leaves about sixty-six hundred in net operating income — and sixty-six hundred on a hundred-ninety-thousand all-in is a cap rate of about three-and-a-half percent, before debt.
Push rent to eleven hundred and trim expenses to forty percent and you're still only around four-point-two. So plainly: pay the median and rent long-term, and the buy-and-hold math is thin — a lower-yield, condition-sensitive play tied to a durable employment base, not a cash-flow machine.
Here's where the LTR actually works: don't pay the median. Land all-in around a hundred fifty thousand instead of a hundred ninety, and that same thousand-a-month rent at forty-five percent expenses moves you to roughly a four-point-four cap, closer to five if you push rent and tighten expenses. So the LTR thesis is real — but only if you control your basis below median, control the rehab, or step up to the play that actually pays for the proximity.

**Play two — the mid-term rental for traveling clinicians. In this pocket, this is the yield angle I'd test first.**
Buy below median, around a hundred twenty, put forty into rehab, then furnish it — eight to twelve thousand more. Call it a hundred seventy thousand all-in including furniture. Furnished mid-term rent to a traveling clinician, verify-day-of, runs roughly fourteen hundred to eighteen hundred a month. Furnished carries heavier costs — utilities, furnishings, turns, vacancy between contracts — so underwrite around fifty percent.
At eighteen hundred that's twenty-one thousand six hundred gross, about ten-thousand-eight in NOI on a hundred-seventy-thousand all-in — a cap rate around six-point-three, the low-to-mid sixes before debt. At fifteen hundred it's closer to five-point-three. The spread that justifies the work is real, but it lives at the top of the rent band, and that band is unverified — confirm it before you commit. The three-to-four-minute drive to the hospital is the moat. Nobody pays a furnished mid-term premium to live twenty minutes out.

**Play three — value-add flip.**
Comps support an after-repair value around one-eighty to two-ten. On South Seventh: eleven-oh-one sold at a hundred twenty thousand, ten-oh-eight square feet, built 1928. Twelve-oh-two at a hundred thirty-nine. Eighteen-fourteen — eight-forty square feet, 1955 — at a hundred fifty-seven. But eleven-oh-two — thirteen-forty-four square feet, 1965 — sold at a hundred ninety-two. Newer and bigger wins; your exit is square footage and finish, not the dirt.
Run the seventy-percent rule hard. On a one-ninety after-repair value with forty in rehab, seventy percent is a hundred thirty-three, minus the forty puts your maximum buy near ninety-three thousand — below the median. That's the discipline: in a ninety-seven-percent sale-to-list market your exit buyer has leverage too, so the flip only pencils if you buy genuinely cheap and the post-rehab square footage carries the comp.

**[4:55–6:05 — THE FOUNDATION RISK = THE MOAT]**
Now the part I refuse to soft-pedal, because if I do, someone buys a money pit on my word.
These homes are pier-and-beam on Bell County black clay — expansive clay that swells when it's wet, shrinks when it's dry, and moves the foundation with it. In today's sample, about four percent of listings flag foundation issues outright — five name it directly — and the real number is almost certainly higher, because foundation problems don't always show up in the remarks. Absence of the word "foundation" in a listing is not clearance. Roughly a third — thirty-seven of a hundred thirteen — mention as-is, rehab, repair, or foundation. That pattern is the neighborhood telling you what it is.
So budget for it: six thousand dollars minimum for foundation work, range five to fifteen depending on severity. And do not lean on a general home inspector for this — hire a structural engineer. Different scope, different liability, different number. Non-negotiable line item.
And it's not just the slab. Pre-1960 stock means galvanized plumbing rusting shut, knob-and-tube or outdated wiring, asbestos and lead in anything pre-1978. That's why the rehab to a livable rental standard is thirty to fifty thousand, not ten.
Here's the reframe, and it's the whole game: the clay is the problem. I won't pretend otherwise. The discount only exists because that problem is real and most people won't touch it. Your edge isn't that the clay is harmless — it's that you can price it correctly when retail buyers and weekend flippers can't. That friction is what keeps the median sub-one-fifty next to a twelve-thousand-person employer. You don't want this easy. You want to be the one who can underwrite the hard part.

If you need it pretty or easy, this neighborhood will punish you.

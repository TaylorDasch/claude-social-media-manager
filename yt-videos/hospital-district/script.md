**[2:20–5:56 — THE PLAYS, WITH TRANSPARENT MATH]**
So how do you make money here? One vocabulary note: when I divide net operating income by your all-in cost, that's yield-on-cost — your unlevered project return. Not a market cap rate, which divides income by market value.

**[2:35] Play one — long-term rental. At the median, this is the worst play on the board, and I'll prove it.**
Buy at the median plus forty thousand of rehab — about a hundred ninety thousand all-in. Long-term rent here, verify-day-of, runs nine hundred to eleven hundred; take a thousand to stay honest. Build the expenses from the line items, because the flat forty-five-percent number people throw around does not survive a seventy-year-old Texas rental. *(Card: tax three thousand, insurance sixteen hundred, management nine, vacancy seven, repairs six, capex four — a sixty-four-percent load → NOI ~$4,300.)* That's a yield-on-cost of about two-point-three percent — unlevered.

Now the part nobody says out loud. Finance it — a hundred-fifty-thousand loan at today's seven-and-a-half to eight-and-a-half percent — and you're negative seven to eight hundred dollars a month after the mortgage. A financed buyer at the median doesn't make thin cash flow; they feed the property every month. Read that right: that negative number isn't a reason to skip the neighborhood — it's the filter. It's why the median is still sub-one-fifty next to a twelve-thousand-person employer: it pushes out everyone who can't bring cash, buy below market, or force value. So where the LTR works: buy near a hundred ten, put the forty in, land around a hundred fifty all-in, and that same eleven-hundred rent moves you to roughly three-point-four percent. Modest, but now you're not bleeding.

**[3:42] Play two — the mid-term rental for traveling clinicians. In this pocket, this is the yield angle I'd test first.**
Same below-median buy, then furnish it — call it a hundred seventy thousand all-in including furniture. Furnished mid-term rent, verify-day-of, is researched at roughly fourteen hundred to eighteen hundred — and the top of that band is the part I have *not* confirmed. Furnished is expensive to run: you cover utilities and internet, management runs higher, you carry gap-vacancy, cleaning, and depreciating furniture. Underwrite a furnished load around sixty-five percent. *(Cards: $1,550/mo → ~3.8% YoC; $1,800/mo → ~4.4% YoC, same load, same all-in.)* So at the mid of the band, the furnished play modestly beats the below-median LTR's three-point-four and clearly beats the median LTR's two-point-three; at the top it pulls clearly ahead. A real edge, not a fantasy spread — but it rests on verifying you can clear that rent. The three-to-four-minute drive gives you a shot at the upper band, but proximity is the reason to test it, not proof that it pays. Confirm the furnished rent before you furnish anything.

**[4:36] Play three — value-add, and this is where the real money lives.**
What the yield math quietly understates: in a value-add play, your return comes from the equity you create on day one, not year-one rent. Comps support an after-repair value around one-ninety. On South Seventh: eighteen-fourteen — eight-forty square feet, 1955 — sold at a hundred fifty-seven, and that's one of mine, so I know that number firsthand. But eleven-oh-two, bigger and newer, sold at a hundred ninety-two. Your exit is square footage and finish, not the dirt.

The one I'd actually run is value-add to *hold* — the forced-equity play. Buy at a hundred twenty, put forty in, you're all-in a hundred sixty against an after-repair value of one-ninety. You just created about thirty thousand dollars of equity — roughly nineteen percent of your all-in — on day one, by fixing the thing nobody else would. Then it compounds: season it, refinance at seventy-five percent of value, pull roughly a hundred forty-two thousand back out, leaving only seventeen, eighteen thousand of your own cash in the deal. From there the tenant — backed by that twelve-thousand-person employer — pays down your loan while you hold for long-run appreciation. *(Flip-to-sell instead? Seventy-percent rule: one-ninety ARV minus forty rehab = max buy near ninety-three thousand, below the median — and your exit buyer has leverage too.)* So the honest answer to "where's the return if the yield is thin?" is the equity you force, the principal someone else pays down, and time — not a fat first-year check. This is a forced-equity hold for a cash or low-leverage operator who can execute a rehab. Anyone selling you a finance-at-the-median cash-flow version is selling you the negative number I just showed you.

**[5:59–7:19 — THE FOUNDATION RISK = THE MOAT]**
someone buys a money pit on my word. These homes are pier-and-beam on Bell County black clay — expansive clay that swells when it's wet, shrinks when it's dry, and moves the foundation with it. A handful of listings flag foundation issues outright, and the real number is almost certainly higher, because the problem doesn't always show up in the remarks. Absence of the word "foundation" is not clearance. Roughly a third of listings mention as-is, rehab, repair, or foundation — that pattern is the neighborhood telling you what it is.

So budget for it: six thousand minimum for foundation work, range five to fifteen. And do not lean on a general home inspector — hire a structural engineer. Different scope, different number. Non-negotiable. And it's not just the slab: pre-1960 stock means galvanized plumbing, outdated wiring, asbestos and lead pre-1978. That's why the rehab is thirty to fifty thousand, not ten.

Here's the reframe, and it's the whole game. The rental demand is already priced in — investors already know about the hospital. So where's your edge? Not information. Execution. The clay is the problem you have to *price correctly* — I won't pretend otherwise. The discount exists because that problem is real and most people won't touch it. Your edge is being the operator who can price and fix the hard part, force the equity, and hold, when retail buyers and weekend flippers can't. You don't want this easy.

Who should avoid it? Anyone financing at the median and expecting cash flow — you'll be writing seven-to-eight-hundred-dollar checks every month. Anyone who wants passive and hands-off on day one.
